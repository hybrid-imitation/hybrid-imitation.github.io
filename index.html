Hybrid Imitation Learning: Teleoperation Augmentation Primitives that Policies Learn to Trigger

Jonne Van Haastregt∗,1, Bastian Orthmann∗,1, Michael C. Welle∗,1,2, Yuchong Zhang2, Danica Kragic2

  • ∗ These authors contributed equally.
  • 1 INCAR Robotics AB, Stockholm, Sweden
  • 2 KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Stockholm, Sweden

Abstract. What an operator can demonstrate bounds what imitation learning can learn. Teleoperation interfaces map the human body to the robot, so motions that are hard for a human, such as holding an exact orientation, returning to the same viewpoint, or turning a wrist joint several full revolutions, are difficult to demonstrate on most teleoperation interfaces, even when they are trivial for the robot. We introduce Teleoperation Augmentation Primitives (TAPs): axis locks, perching waypoints, pose anchors, and embodiment-specific routines that the operator triggers during a demonstration by speech, an AR menu, or simply with a controller button. TAPs are themselves recorded in the demonstration and can therefore also be learned and invoked by the policy itself. In simulation, where benchmarks provide human demonstrations, we show that augmenting them with TAPs after the fact yields better policies on a wrist-camera-only peg insertion (0.372 to 0.531 success) and on a mug-cleanup task with a learned “remember this pose” anchor (0.203 to 0.323). On a real robot, three tasks show the same pattern: primitives that help the operator do not hurt the policy, and a policy triggering an unscrewing routine succeeds where a plain policy cannot resolve the multi-turn motion from images alone (67% vs. 38% success). We close with an industrial proof-of-concept case leveraging this idea.

Overview. During teleoperation the operator triggers a Teleoperation Augmentation Primitive (TAP) by speech or (AR) controller input; the press and the primitive's motion are recorded with the demonstration. The policy is trained with a discrete primitive channel next to its continuous action and learns to trigger the same primitives at inference time. The mechanism is policy-agnostic: in this work the channel is carried by Diffusion Policy, a behavioural-cloning MLP (BC) and a causal-Transformer behavioural-cloning policy (BC-Transformer).

Teleoperation Augmentation Primitives

A TAP is a tuple (trigger, routine, parameters): a trigger (the operator's press during demonstration; the policy's channel at execution), a routine that takes over the robot from the trigger until it terminates, and parameters that come from static configuration, from anchors recorded by the operator, or from the robot's proprioception at the trigger. We use four types of TAPs:

  • Axis locks. Adding rotation to translation is hard for a human hand. An axis lock fixes one or more of {x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw} in the tool or base frame: the operator reaches a desired orientation, locks the rotational axes, and completes the translation without holding the orientation by hand; locking x and y gives a pure vertical insertion.
  • Perching waypoints. A pre-set pose the operator returns to when useful: it brings the scene back into the eye-in-hand camera, so no stationary camera is needed, and splits a long task into shorter segments with a common start.
  • Anchors. A zero-motion primitive (“remember this pose”): the press records the current end-effector pose, and a later primitive executes relative to it. Anchors are how a learned policy can dynamically integrate information into a routine or a perching waypoint.
  • Routines. Embodiment-specific motions the operator's body cannot perform through the interface, such as rotating a wrist joint through several full revolutions. The unscrewing routine is close → rotate → open → rotate back → close → rotate → lift, parameterised by the repetitions and the rotation per step. Routines are open-loop in this paper; a closed-loop controller or a sub-policy can be triggered the same way.

TAP types. (a) Vial aspiration: the operator locks the rotational axes once the needle is aligned. (b) Open-container liquid transfer: perching waypoints bring the source and the target container into the eye-in-hand view. (c) Container unscrewing: an unscrewing routine grasps, unscrews and lifts the cap. (d) MugCleanup (simulated): an anchor press stores the drawer frame while the open gripper sits behind the handle; after the mug is grasped, a carry routine transports it into that frame and shuts the drawer.

Triggering during teleoperation

TAPs are triggered on a Quest-based teleoperation interface by speech (the operator names one of the predefined commands, an embedded Whisper model transcribes it and the closest command executes, with vibration patterns confirming start, end and rejection), through an AR menu over the controller in the headset, or by haptic buttons: experienced operators trigger the same menu blind, with vibration patterns telling them which menu is active. Every press is logged with the demonstration as a channel per primitive.

The primitive channel

With K primitives available, a channel pt ∈ {−1, +1}K is appended to the robot action and the policy is trained on the augmented action [xt, pt]. While a routine runs, the executed motion is recorded as ordinary actions, so a policy trained without the channel on the same data sees the routine as demonstrated motion. Any policy that regresses an action chunk can regress the channel. At execution a primitive is triggered on a rising edge of its channel while no primitive is active; the primitive takes over until it terminates while the policy keeps predicting, then the policy takes control back.

Simulation study

The simulation study asks whether demonstrations with TAPs produce a better policy than without. Since the demonstrations come from benchmarks that predate TAPs, the unassisted control is unbiased. We start from human teleoperation datasets in robosuite: the proficient-human Square demonstrations of robomimic and a seeded subset of the MugCleanup D1 demonstrations of MimicGen. A demonstration is replayed from its recorded state up to the point an operator should have activated a TAP, the primitive is executed from that state with observations rendered online, and the demonstration is stitched back into the unassisted one afterwards. The unassisted demonstrations are therefore an identical control: same demonstration names, same split; the only difference is the TAP.

  • Square (wrist camera only, no proprioception): the robot grasps a square nut and threads it onto a peg that is out of view while the nut is grasped. The TAP is a perching waypoint above the peg; three waypoints cover the three ways operators grasp the handle. After the perch, the policy performs the insertion itself.
  • MugCleanup (wrist camera + proprioception): open a drawer, place a mug upright inside, shut the drawer. Two TAPs: an anchor press with the open gripper behind the handle, the last step before the drawer moves, and a carry routine that, once the mug is grasped, transports it into the anchored drawer frame and shuts the drawer.

Data effect or trigger effect?

To separate what the TAP data contributes from what the learned trigger contributes, we also train on the TAP demonstrations without the channel: the policy sees the routine's motion as demonstrated motion and runs alone at execution, like the unassisted policy.

Square0.3720.2970.531

On MugCleanup about a third of the gain is in the data, the regular carry being easier to imitate than the operators' own transport, and two thirds come from the learned hand-over. On Square the TAP data on its own costs: the no-channel policy must imitate the perch routine from the wrist image alone, without the end-effector pose the routine itself servos on, and falls below the unassisted control; with the channel the same demonstrations give the best policy.

TAPs across policy classes

The primitive channel is an extra regression target, so any policy class whose loss can treat it as one can carry it. Real-robot validation

The real-robot experiments validate the full system end to end on a ViperX 300 S arm with a single eye-in-hand camera and no stationary camera: demonstrations collected with the operator triggering TAPs, a policy that learns to trigger the same TAPs, and rollouts on novel initial conditions. Unlike in simulation, every real demonstration was collected with TAPs, and both policies are trained on that data. Ours is Hybrid Imitation Learning instantiated on Diffusion Policy (Hybrid-Diffusion), triggering the primitives it was given; the baseline (DP) is the same Diffusion Policy on the same demonstrations without the primitive channel, so it sees each primitive's motion as demonstrated actions and must reproduce it from its observations. The comparison therefore isolates the learned trigger. For each task we sample seven initial configurations with the same seed for both policies and execute each three times, 21 rollouts per policy and task.

In no task does the TAP-triggering policy perform worse than its counterpart. On vial aspiration and liquid transfer the two perform alike: the axis lock and the perching waypoints made the demonstrations clean for both policies, and a policy trained on clean demonstrations can learn to hold an orientation, or to return to a vantage point, without an explicit trigger. On unscrewing the upside appears: the baseline turns the cap once or twice and then lifts while the cap is still threaded, since a closed cap and a cap turned once look nearly identical in the images; the routine turns a fixed number of times, so the hybrid policy removed the cap in every rollout in which it triggered the routine from the right position.

The operator positions the gripper over the cap and triggers the unscrewing routine, which grasps the cap, unscrews it and lifts it off, after which the cap is placed on the table. The baseline removes the cap in 8 of 21 rollouts and the hybrid policy in 14.

Deployment: policy-triggered routines for clutch assembly

We applied the same decomposition to an industrial clutch-assembly task as a proof of concept: stacking the discs onto the clutch shaft of a multi-plate clutch. One clutch assembly is 35 discs of three types (a spring disc that can face up or down, a friction disc, and a steel disc), stacked onto a shaft whose rotation is not fixed, mounted on a tray whose position is only roughly known. A UR16e with a compliant two-finger gripper and two eye-in-hand cameras (no scene camera) retrieves the discs from three magazines, which an operator loads in random orientations.

Four routines can be triggered to retrieve the discs. Each routine retrieves its disc from the magazine and brings it to a perch point over the workspace from which the tray is in view; the policy then performs the insertion and retreats, and the next routine is triggered. The demonstrations were collected with the routines triggered by the operator, who teleoperated only the insertion of each disc: 15 complete assemblies, i.e. 525 demonstrated insertions. The policy's best runs assembled two complete clutches, 70 consecutive insertions, without an interruption.
